Source: Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha & Ors., (1995) 6 SCC 651, Supreme Court of India,
13 November 1995 (judgment: https://digiscr.sci.gov.in/admin/judgement_file/judgement_pdf/1995/Supp.%20(5)/Part%20I/S_1995_110-151_1702288886.pdf);
consumer complaints are filed online on the e-Jagriti portal of the Department of Consumer Affairs,
launched 1 January 2025 to bring together e-Daakhil and the earlier systems (https://e-jagriti.gov.in;
https://en.vikaspedia.in/viewcontent/social-welfare/social-awareness/consumer-education/e-jagriti-portal-for-consumer-grievance-redressal).
Key fact: The Supreme Court held that medical treatment by doctors and hospitals is a "service" under
consumer protection law, so a patient who paid for treatment (or whose treatment was paid for, for example
by an insurer or employer) can complain to a Consumer Commission about deficient service and may be
awarded compensation. A token registration fee does not make free treatment a paid service.
Key fact: Complaints go to the District Consumer Commission when the amount paid is up to ₹50 lakh, and
can be filed online on e-Jagriti.
Caveat: Limits of this advice: treatment given completely free to everyone, such as free care at a
government hospital, was held not to be a paid "service", so a consumer complaint may not apply there. In
2024 the Supreme Court (Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi) asked a larger bench to reconsider this
judgment; until it rules, the 1995 judgment applies. Whether a case can win depends on its facts: never
promise compensation, and suggest free legal aid or a lawyer.
Use this when the person paid a private hospital, was refused emergency treatment or got poor treatment
there, and asks whether they can complain, claim compensation or "sue".
